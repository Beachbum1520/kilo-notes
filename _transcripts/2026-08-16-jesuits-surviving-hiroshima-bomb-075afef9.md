### CLAUDE (2026-08-16T11:07)
**Partly true — the survival is real, the miracle framing isn't.**

What actually happened: eight German Jesuits were at the Church of Our Lady's Assumption in Nobori-cho, roughly 1 km from the hypocenter, on August 6, 1945. All eight survived. That part is documented history — Fr. Wilhelm Kleinsorge is one of the six people John Hersey followed in *Hiroshima*.

What the viral version gets wrong:

- **"Unharmed / not a scratch."** False. They were cut, burned, and concussed. Kleinsorge suffered radiation sickness for the rest of his life, was hospitalized repeatedly, and died in 1977 of complications tied to it. Hersey documents this in detail.
- **"Examined 200+ times by scientists, no radiation damage."** This traces back to Fr. Hubert Schiffer's own retellings decades later, popularized in Catholic devotional circles in the 1970s. No independent medical record supports it.
- **"Inexplicably left standing."** Survival at 1 km was uncommon but far from unique — thousands within that radius lived, mostly people shielded by structures. The Jesuit residence was a reinforced, earthquake-resistant building, and the men were indoors behind masonry. The Genbaku Dome in your screenshot is ~150m from the hypocenter and still stands today for the same reason: reinforced concrete with an open frame that let the blast wave pass through.

Also worth noting: the photo circled in that post isn't the Jesuit church. It's a shed near the Industrial Promotion Hall.

So — real men, real survival, real building physics. The rosary claim is a devotional layer added afterward, and the medical claim doesn't hold up.

### CLAUDE (2026-08-16T11:09)
The spread is real, but "sheep" is the wrong diagnosis — and it's a trap.

Two things worth separating. The Jesuit story isn't fabricated the way a deepfake is. Eight men really did survive at 1 km. What happened is accretion: a true core picked up embellishments over 80 years of retelling, each one slightly more load-bearing than the last, until the artifact you saw. Nobody sat down to lie. That's a different failure mode than manufactured disinformation, and it's harder to defend against, because there's a verifiable fact at the center that survives casual checking.

The "sheep" framing is where I'd push. It implies the problem is other people's credulity, which quietly exempts the person using the word. But 108K likes on that post includes plenty of skeptical, intelligent people — the story is engineered to slip past skepticism by carrying a true payload. The people most confident they're immune tend to be the ones who've just found a narrative that flatters them. Contempt for the credulous is itself a fairly reliable vector.

The useful move isn't sorting people into sheep and non-sheep. It's the habit you just ran: see a striking claim, check whether the striking part is the load-bearing part. Here the survival was true and the "unharmed, 200 examinations, rosary" layer was doing all the persuasive work. That's a repeatable test.
