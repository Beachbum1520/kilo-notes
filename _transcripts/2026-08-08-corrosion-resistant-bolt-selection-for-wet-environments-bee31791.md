### CLAUDE (2026-08-08T12:45)
A drop-in anchor is a hollow metal sleeve with a small tapered plug (an expander cone) sitting loose in the bottom. Dropping it in the hole does nothing by itself — you have to drive that plug up into the sleeve to flare it against the concrete. The setting tool is the steel punch that does the driving.

It's a solid steel rod with a shoulder machined into it. You drop the anchor in the hole, set the tool into the anchor's mouth, and hit the tool with a hammer 3–5 times until the shoulder bottoms out flush on the anchor's lip. That shoulder is the whole point: it stops you at exactly the right depth so you can't over-drive and split the sleeve, or under-drive and leave a loose anchor. When the shoulder is touching, you're done.

They're size-specific — a 3/8" anchor needs a 3/8" setting tool, a 1/2" needs a 1/2". Around $8–12 at a hardware store, or often bundled with a box of anchors. One tool sets as many as you want.

This is the thing wedge anchors don't need, which is their main advantage: you drop a wedge anchor in and tighten the nut, and the tightening expands it. No extra tool. The tradeoff is the one you already know — a wedge anchor is permanent, and a drop-in lets you unbolt and lift the waterer off the pad.

**The governing fact: almost nothing bonds to polyethylene.** It's a low-surface-energy plastic — that's why nothing sticks to a cutting board. So don't shop for an adhesive. You want a **gasket**, something that stays pliable and seals by compression under the weight of the base, not by gluing to it.

**First choice: non-hardening butyl rubber sealant.** It never fully cures, stays tacky and flexible for decades, tolerates the poly expanding and contracting against static concrete, and seals fine without needing a chemical bond. **Second choice: polyurethane** (Sika 1a, Sonolastic NP1). Bonds concrete beautifully, poly poorly, but it stays flexible and works as a seal. Tougher and more abrasion-resistant than butyl if cattle are shoving on that skirt.

**Avoid:** acrylic latex — hardens, cracks, fails outdoors in a year. And construction adhesive like PL Premium, which won't grab the poly anyway and only makes a mess.

Why this matters more than it looks: on an energy-free waterer, the seal is the whole mechanism. The unit works by trapping ground heat rising through that 3/4" riser inside the insulated shell. An air leak under the skirt lets that heat wash out and you get ice in January.
