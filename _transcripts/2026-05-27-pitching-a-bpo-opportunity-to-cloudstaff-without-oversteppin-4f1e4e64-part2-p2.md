The purpose is to protect the company from someone joining, taking equity, and leaving in 6 months. It aligns the person to stick around at least a year before they've earned anything.

**Why it matters for you specifically:** you're not a random hire — you're the person the venture is built around. A 1-year cliff on someone in your position is standard-boilerplate, but it's the wrong shape for what you're actually doing. You'd want to negotiate one of:

- **No cliff, straight monthly vesting from day one.** Most aggressive ask. Reasonable for a founder-equivalent.
- **Shorter cliff (3 or 6 months).** Middle ground.
- **Cliff with a specified acceleration on certain events** — e.g., if you're terminated without cause, or if Cloudstaff sells the vertical, the cliff is deemed satisfied.

The other vesting piece worth knowing: **acceleration provisions.** Two kinds worth negotiating:

- **Single-trigger acceleration:** if the vertical is sold, your unvested equity accelerates and vests immediately. You get paid on the exit even if you haven't hit the timeline.
- **Double-trigger acceleration:** if the vertical is sold AND you're subsequently terminated within X months, unvested accelerates. More common, less protective.

You want single-trigger for a change of control if you can get it. Cloudstaff will push double-trigger. Meeting in the middle is common.

## Anti-dilution — the Saverin comparison is exactly the right instinct, and it's more than one provision

Saverin got diluted from ~30% to ~0.03% in Facebook. What happened to him is a real thing that happens to founding operators when they're not sophisticated about the paper they signed. Good instinct to flag it. But "anti-dilution" is actually a bundle of protections, and knowing which ones you need is the difference between real protection and a false sense of it.

**Three things can dilute you, and they need three different protections:**

**1. New capital raises.** Cloudstaff (or the vertical) raises another round. New investors get equity, which comes out of the existing cap table. Everyone including you gets a smaller slice of a bigger pie.

*Protection:* **Weighted-average anti-dilution** — the standard, and what you should ask for. If new shares are issued at a lower valuation than what your equity was priced at, your equity converts at a favorable ratio so you're partially protected. There's also "full ratchet" anti-dilution, which is more aggressive (converts you 1:1), but you rarely get that as an operator — it's usually a VC-only protection.

*What "no dilution" actually means, though — and here's where I have to push back gently on your framing:* you probably don't want *pure* no-dilution. If Cloudstaff raises capital to fund your growth, that capital creates value you benefit from. Blocking all dilution would block them from funding the vertical, which is against your interests. What you want is protection against *unfair* dilution — dilution at bad valuations, or dilution that's structured to squeeze you out specifically. That's what weighted-average anti-dilution gives you.

**2. Employee/option pool expansion.** As the vertical grows, Cloudstaff will need to grant equity to new senior hires. That equity has to come from somewhere — and it usually gets carved out of everyone's stake proportionally.

*Protection:* **Pool carve-out negotiated up front.** You agree to a reserved option pool of, say, 10% of the vertical, established at closing. Any grants beyond that pool require your consent. This prevents "we need to grant more equity to attract talent" from being used to erode you.

**3. The Saverin scenario specifically — deliberate dilution via new share issuance to specific parties.** This is what happened to him: the company issued a bunch of new shares to Zuck and others at essentially no cost, which massively diluted Saverin because he wasn't in the meeting that authorized it.

*Protection:* **Preemptive rights + voting rights on share issuance.** Preemptive rights mean any new shares issued have to be offered to you first at the same terms, so you can maintain your percentage by buying in. Voting rights on share issuance mean issuance requires a threshold of shareholders to approve — you either need to be on the approval or need the threshold high enough that you can't be routed around.

**The Saverin-proof combination you want:**
- Weighted-average anti-dilution on price
- Preemptive rights on new issuances
- Voting rights (or board seat) on major share issuances and cap table changes
- Pre-agreed option pool carve-out
- Information rights (regular financial statements, cap table updates) so you can *see* dilution happening

That combination doesn't stop dilution — nothing entirely does — but it stops you from being blindsided or squeezed out unilaterally. Which is what actually happened to Saverin. He didn't get diluted; he got *routed around* because he wasn't at the table when it happened.
