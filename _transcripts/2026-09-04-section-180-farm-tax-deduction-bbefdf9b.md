### CLAUDE (2026-09-04T14:01)

**Section 180** lets a farmer immediately deduct the value of *residual soil fertility* (excess phosphorus, potassium, lime, etc.) present in farmland at the time you acquire it — instead of treating it as part of the land's capitalized basis. It's been in the tax code since 1960, recently popularized by firms doing soil-testing/valuation services. Typical claimed values run $750–$2,000/acre.

**The critical qualifier for you: timing.** The election is only available *in the year you acquire the land*, on a timely-filed return (extensions count). It doesn't apply to land you've farmed for years — only to residual fertility left behind by a *previous owner* that you're now "buying." If Watts Way's Franklin acreage wasn't acquired this tax year, this deduction isn't available for it. Missed years generally can't be fixed retroactively — the IRS hasn't confirmed a path via amended return, and most advisors won't rely on one.

Other requirements if you're inside the acquisition-year window:
- Soil tests must be done post-closing, pre-application of any new fertilizer, to isolate what the prior owner left vs. baseline
- Fertility must be demonstrably tied to the prior owner's actual fertilizer applications — not just "whatever nutrients happen to be in the dirt" (this is where aggressive promoters have drawn IRS/CALT scrutiny — valuing *all* soil nutrients rather than only traceable residual fertilizer is not well-supported)
- You need an agronomist to document the excess-over-baseline value; a CPA to support the position
- Nitrogen is contested — many advisors exclude it since it cycles annually rather than persisting

**Bottom line for your situation:** if you're not in the acquisition year of a land purchase, this isn't a lever available to you right now — it's a one-time, point-of-purchase election, not an ongoing deduction. If you're planning to buy additional acreage for the farm, this is worth lining up soil tests *before* the closing, so ask then rather than after the fact.
