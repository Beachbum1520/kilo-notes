Here's what's going on. The shipping fee you paid the seller covered *transport* — getting the box to your door. Import duty is a separate charge assessed by US Customs at the border, and it's owed by the importer of record, which is the person receiving the goods: you. Unless the seller explicitly sold them "duty paid" / DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) — meaning their checkout promised no import charges — duties were never part of what you paid them. Carriers like FedEx routinely clear the package, front the duty to Customs to avoid delaying delivery, then bill you afterward. So a bill showing up after the shirts arrived is normal, not a billing error.

The reason this is even happening — when a small shirt order years ago probably wouldn't have triggered anything — is that the US eliminated the $800 "de minimis" exemption. China and Hong Kong lost it first, effective May 2, 2025, and then effective August 29, 2025, the exemption was suspended for shipments from all other countries. All imports into the US now require formal customs entry and payment of applicable duties regardless of value. Apparel also carries relatively high duty rates to begin with, plus a 10% global surcharge now layered on top, so even a modest order can generate a real bill.

Before you pay, a few things worth checking:

- **Get the itemized breakdown.** FedEx bills often combine the actual duty/tax (which goes to Customs) with their own "advancement" or "disbursement fee" for fronting the money. The duty itself you owe; the disbursement fee is sometimes negotiable or waivable, especially for a one-off.
- **Check the declared value and classification.** If the value FedEx declared is inflated, or the goods were misclassified, the duty is overstated. You can dispute that.
- **Re-read the seller's checkout/terms.** If they advertised "import duties included" or "no customs fees," that doesn't get FedEx off your back, but it gives you a claim against the *seller* to reimburse you.

The fact that you gave the shirts away rather than resold them doesn't change anything — duty attaches at import, regardless of what you do with the goods after.

One practical note: refusing a legitimate FedEx duty bill usually just sends it to collections and can flag your account for future shipments, so unless the breakdown shows a clear error, it's generally not worth fighting the duty portion itself. The disbursement fee is the part most worth a phone call.
