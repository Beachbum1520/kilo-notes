### CLAUDE (2026-07-31T02:01)
Default posture: yes, with structure — not yes, and not "let me think about it." During a merger, hesitation reads as limited appetite, and appetite is what gets evaluated when the org chart gets redrawn. But "yes" without structure is how interim becomes permanent at your current comp and title.

**Say in the room:**

- Accept the work directly, in one sentence, then move immediately to scoping. Don't make him sell it to you.
- Ask whether this is caretaker coverage or permanent ownership, and if he doesn't know, ask for a date when it gets decided. Undefined interim is the trap — it defaults to permanent by inertia and you lose the moment to re-level.
- Ask what comes with it: headcount, budget lines, vendor contracts, and whether direct reports move to you or dotted-line. Scope without authority over the resources is accountability you can't execute on.
- Ask what you're allowed to slow down. You have CTC compliance, two live RFPs, the Mitel-to-PhoneSuite transition, 110S, and the PNOC pass-rate target. Make him name the deprioritization rather than absorbing it silently — that's the record you want when Q4 results land.
- Ask for a diligence window — 2–3 weeks to inventory Joe's open commitments, escalations, and vendor obligations before you own the outcomes. You do not want to inherit an unknown liability the way the Grand Hyatt Vail suppression surfaced.

**Don't raise comp tomorrow.** Raising it in the same conversation makes the yes conditional and cheapens both asks. Signal it instead: "Assuming this holds past the interim period, I'd want to sit down on level and scope separately." Then book that conversation once the scope is actually defined and you have a headcount/P&L number to point at. Jady owns that decision; give him the clean routing he prefers.

**The one thing to watch:** if any of Joe's scope touches Kathy's territory, don't let the handoff be negotiated between you and her. Get Jady to define the boundary in the same conversation where he hands you the work, or you'll be litigating it for six months.

What does Joe actually own? The answer changes materially depending on whether it's technical delivery, a customer-facing function, or something that overlaps sales.
