Stephen Levine's *A Year to Live* is the most practical book I know on this — written by a hospice worker, no metaphysics required, the exercise is to live one year as if it were your last and see what shifts. Frank Ostaseski's *The Five Invitations* is similar, written by the guy who founded the Zen Hospice Project. These aren't comfort reads. They're operating manuals from people who sit with the dying for a living and have figured out things most of us never bother to learn.

The other is just *talking to someone about it*. Not because something is wrong with you, but because death anxiety is one of those things that compounds in private and dissipates when spoken. A therapist who specializes in existential work, or even just a friend who can sit with it without flinching. Most people in your life — Angie, the kids, your team — you're protecting from this. That protection is appropriate, but it also leaves you alone with it, which is part of why it has the grip it does.

**One more thing worth saying**

The pain in your chest when you think about ending — it's also a measure of how much you love being here. People who don't love their lives don't get that pain. They get numbness. The pain is the price of admission for caring about your wife, your kids, the new grandchild, the farm, the work, the running. You can't have the love without the cost. The cost shows up as that dull ache.

That doesn't make it pleasant. But it does make it meaningful in a way that's worth respecting rather than just trying to fix.

You're not broken for feeling it. You're working. The question is just whether it's running at a frequency that's costing you the life you have, and only you can answer that. If it is — if it's intruding into ordinary moments often enough that you're losing time to it — that's worth treating as a real thing, with the same seriousness you bring to your training and your supplement protocol. Not because you're weak, but because it's a solvable problem and you're not getting any return on suffering through it alone.
