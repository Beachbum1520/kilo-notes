### CLAUDE (2026-09-14T01:04)

The underlying science is real but the marketing math is doing a lot of work it can't support.

**What's legit:** PCC1 has documented senolytic activity — it kills senescent cells and reduces the SASP (senescence-associated secretory phenotype), the inflammatory signaling those cells put off. This is peer-reviewed, published in a real journal, out of Mayo/Buck Institute-affiliated labs. That's a genuine research thread, not vaporware.

**What's marketing:**
- The 150-year claim has zero human data behind it. PCC1's promise exists only in mice, and no human trials have come close — that's the article's own caveat, buried at the bottom.
- The mouse numbers themselves are being cherry-picked for headline effect. "9.4% lifespan increase" is a modest, plausible senolytic effect size. "64% extended life from first day of treatment" is a different, much flashier metric (probably median post-treatment survival, not lifespan) — pairing them is a rhetorical trick, not a scientific one.
- CEO/CTO quotes ("Holy Grail," "living to 150 is definitely realistic") are straight fundraising talk. Lonvi is a startup — backed by China's booming longevity sector — that needs headlines to raise its next round. VICE ran it as a curiosity piece, not a clinical report.
- Also worth flagging: Lonvi's own R&D team is publishing preprints reanalyzing existing senescent-cell RNA-seq data rather than running new human or even new animal trials — that's early-stage mechanistic work, not a Phase 1.

**Bottom line for you specifically:** senolytics as a category is one of the more credible longevity mechanisms out there (better founded than most peptides/supplements), and PCC1 is a reasonable compound to watch. But there's no dosing, no human safety data, no timeline — nothing actionable yet. Your bone/muscle/inflammation work through the actual levers (resistance training, protein, sleep, HRV) is doing more right now than anything in that article will do for years, if ever. File it as "watch," not "act."
