### CLAUDE (2026-07-21T02:58)
"Memento mori" is Latin for "remember that you must die" — literally, "remember death."

It's an ancient concept, popularized by the Stoics, meant as a reminder of mortality — not to be morbid, but to sharpen focus on living well now. The idea shows up throughout history: Roman generals supposedly had a servant whisper it during triumphal parades, medieval art used skulls and hourglasses as memento mori symbols, and Marcus Aurelius returned to the theme repeatedly in *Meditations*.

The practical thrust is that keeping death in view strips away trivial concerns and clarifies what actually matters — a filter for priorities, essentially.

### CLAUDE (2026-07-21T03:00)
The concept of amor fati captures the idea of embracing one's fate rather than resisting it.That's "amor fati" — Latin for "love of fate."

It's the companion idea to memento mori, most associated with Nietzsche and the Stoics. It means not just accepting what happens to you — including suffering and loss — but embracing it as necessary, even willing it. Nietzsche described it as wanting nothing to be different, not backward, not forward, not in all eternity.

The Stoic version, via Epictetus and Marcus Aurelius, is more practical: you don't control what happens, only your response, so wishing events were otherwise is wasted energy. Loving your fate turns every obstacle into material to work with rather than something to resent.

Together the two make a neat pair: memento mori tells you time is short; amor fati tells you not to waste it fighting what you can't change.
