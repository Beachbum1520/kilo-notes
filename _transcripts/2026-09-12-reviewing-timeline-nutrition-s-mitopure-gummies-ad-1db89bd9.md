### CLAUDE (2026-09-12T18:46)

That link is a Facebook ad landing page for Timeline Nutrition's Mitopure gummies (Urolithin A supplement) — the URL parameters tell you a lot before you even click: it's a paid Facebook campaign using a "pharmacist reaction" UGC-style video, targeting "proactive midlifers," pointed at a gummies product page.

On the product itself:

- **Real science, oversold framing.** Urolithin A is a genuine postbiotic compound studied for its effect on mitophagy (cellular recycling of damaged mitochondria). There's legitimate peer-reviewed research behind it — a 2022 randomized trial (Singh et al., *Cell Rep Med*) showed modest muscle strength gains in middle-aged adults over 16 weeks.
- **The marketing outruns the data.** Claims like "targets signs of aging at the source" and "renew aging cells" are supplement-marketing language layered on top of narrower clinical findings (the actual trial showed up to 12% muscle strength improvement in *sedentary* middle-aged adults after 16 weeks — not the more sweeping anti-aging framing on the page).
- **"Pharmacist reaction" UGC ads** are a common trust-borrowing tactic — someone in scrubs reacting with surprise to a product tends to test well and implies clinical authority the person may not actually be providing an independent review.
- Nestlé Health Science and L'Oréal are investors, so this isn't a fly-by-night operation, but that also means there's serious ad spend behind normalizing this as a daily-use longevity product.

Bottom line: Urolithin A has real, if modest, clinical support for muscle function in middle-aged/older adults — worth knowing if you're evaluating it on the merits. But the ad itself is textbook direct-response supplement marketing (authority-borrowing testimonial, urgency-free but curiosity-driven creative, "proactive midlifer" targeting), so treat the landing page copy as marketing, not a clinical summary, and check the actual studies if you're deciding whether to buy it.

Given your actual stack, no — Urolithin A gummies wouldn't add much for you specifically, and you already have the pieces that matter:

- You're on **TRT (9u/day)** — this alone dwarfs anything Urolithin A does for muscle. TRT is a direct anabolic driver; UA's effect size in the trials is small (that ~12% strength gain was in *sedentary, untreated* midlife adults over 16 weeks — a much lower bar than someone already training on a structured 4-day split with exogenous testosterone).
- **Creatine 10g/day** — the actual highest-evidence, largest-effect-size muscle/strength supplement that exists. You're already at a solid dose.
- **DHEA, NMN** — you're already running adjacent longevity/mitochondrial-support compounds. UA overlaps in mechanism (mitochondrial biogenesis/mitophagy) without adding much beyond what you're already doing metabolically.

**The honest read on Urolithin A itself:**
The mitophagy mechanism is real and mildly interesting, but the clinical effect size on muscle strength is modest and was demonstrated in people who were *not* already resistance training and *not* on TRT. Stacking it on top of your current protocol, you'd be adding a compound whose primary studied benefit (mitochondrial energy production in undertrained muscle) is already being addressed by training + creatine + TRT — the marginal return is likely to round to zero and be hard to distinguish from noise in your DEXA/strength numbers.

**Where it might actually make sense:** if you were optimizing pure longevity/cellular-health markers independent of muscle (its mitophagy angle has more standalone rationale there), or if Bailey specifically wanted another mitochondrial-support lever. But for "adding muscle" as the stated goal, it's not where I'd spend money — creatine dose optimization, protein timing, or progressive overload adherence will move the needle further than a $60-90/month gummy stack.
