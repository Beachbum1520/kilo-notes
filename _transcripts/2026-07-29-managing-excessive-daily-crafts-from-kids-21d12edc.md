### CLAUDE (2026-07-29T22:47)

You probably can't stop it, and going after the output directly is the wrong lever. Here's the read you didn't ask for: 24-30 items a day from three kids aged 9, 11, and 12 isn't craft enthusiasm. Nine-year-olds make things. Twelve-year-olds who make you eight cards a day are running a check. Six years in from 2020, and the currency they've settled on is "produce evidence, get confirmation." Cut the channel without replacing it and you get escalation first, then withdrawal. Withdrawal is quieter, which will feel like a win for about four months.

What actually moves the number:

- **Constrain the inputs, not the output.** The foil, the construction paper, the tape — put it in a bin with a designated supply and a craft window. No confrontation, no "stop making me things," just less raw material and a time when it happens.
- **Make the fridge finite.** Three slots each. To add one, they pull one. They choose. This teaches editing and it caps your storage and guilt problem permanently.
- **Stop paying for volume.** Fake enthusiasm 30 times a day is what's inflating supply — and they can tell it's fake, which is partly why they keep going. Pick one item, spend ninety real seconds on it, ask a specific question about it. Ignore the rest without comment.
- **Photograph and dispose.** Phone album, then the trash. Solves the physical accumulation without a scene.
- **Redirect the drive at a target that isn't you.** Cards mailed to grandparents. Signage or labels for Watts Way. A bound book they work on over weeks. Something with a recipient or a deadline absorbs a lot of that output.

The second-order thing worth sitting with: the volume drops on its own when they stop needing the receipt. If it doesn't drop as you decouple your reaction from quantity — or if one of them escalates hard — that's information about which kid is least sure of their footing, and it's worth more to you than a clean kitchen counter.
