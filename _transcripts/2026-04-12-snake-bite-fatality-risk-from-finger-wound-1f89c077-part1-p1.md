### CLAUDE (2026-04-12T23:32)

Here's the honest risk picture, Scott:

**Overall fatality odds in the US are very low.** About 7,000 people are bitten by venomous snakes annually in the US, and only 0.2% — roughly 1 in 500 — result in death. Of those 7,000–8,000 annual bites, only about five people die each year.

**The "baby snake" factor is a common myth worth addressing.** Baby venomous snakes are not more dangerous than adults in terms of venom potency — adults actually carry more venom. However, juveniles can be harder to identify, which increases risk of mishandling.

**Finger bites specifically:** The most common site of snakebite injury is the distal upper extremity — wrist, hand, or fingers account for 44% of all snakebite cases. Finger bites are actually the most survivable due to less vascular tissue and slower systemic absorption compared to torso or neck bites.

**Species matters most.** Rattlesnakes accounted for over 90% of identifiable fatal bites. There were only five fatalities attributed to copperheads and two due to cottonmouths over a 30-year period.

**Bottom line:** If this actually happened — get to an ER regardless. Don't try to identify or capture the snake. Death following envenomation from a native U.S. snake is unlikely, particularly if medical attention is sought promptly. The bite location (finger) and small snake size both work in your favor, but "baby snake" doesn't rule out venomous, and a finger bite can still cause significant tissue damage even without systemic death risk.
