**Why these ratios:** Corn drives the energy density you need for fat deposition and marbling on Dexters. A common starting point producers use is roughly 60% corn / 30% hull or fiber source / 10% protein, working corn up to 80–85% on free-choice over the finishing period. Cottonseed hulls give you the roughage you need to keep the rumen healthy on a high-grain diet — they should stay between 10–25% of the diet for finishing cattle, and beet pulp adds highly digestible fiber that works well as a supplemental energy source in finishing rations. **A few important things to flag before next week:**

1. **Step them up gradually.** Don't drop them on this mix cold from pasture — start at maybe 4–5 lb/head/day and work up over 2–3 weeks to avoid acidosis. Free-choice good hay alongside while transitioning.

2. **Mineral is non-negotiable.** This mix has no added Ca, salt, or trace minerals. You need a quality finisher mineral free-choice or top-dressed — corn is high in phosphorus and the Ca:P ratio will be way out of whack without it.

3. **Skip cottonseed for any bulls.** Gossypol in cottonseed products can hit bull fertility — keep Chester (or any breeding bulls) off this mix.

4. **Dexters finish small and fast.** Given they're a smaller-framed breed, watch body condition closely — they can over-finish quicker than you'd expect on a hot ration like this.

### CLAUDE (2026-04-26T02:21)
 **Perilla mint (*Perilla frutescens*) is one of the most toxic plants to cattle in the Southeast** — and Georgia is right in the heart of its range.

**What it looks like:**

- Square stems (it's in the mint family)
- Opposite leaves, oval with serrated edges, often with a purplish underside or fully purple variety
- Grows 1–3 feet tall
- Crinkly, slightly puckered leaf texture
- Strong minty/musky smell when crushed (some people say it smells like a cross between mint and lemon)
- Small white-to-purple flowers on spikes in late summer/early fall
- Often confused with basil or coleus at first glance

**Where it grows:**

- Shaded areas — fence lines, woodland edges, under trees
- Creek banks and damp bottoms
- Disturbed soil, old barn lots, around outbuildings
- Anywhere cattle loaf in the shade is prime habitat

**Why it's dangerous:**

It contains ketones (perilla ketone, primarily) that cause **acute bovine pulmonary edema and emphysema** — also called "fog fever" or "panters." The lungs literally fill with fluid. Cattle can die within hours of ingestion, often with no warning signs until they're already in respiratory distress (rapid breathing, open-mouth breathing, foam at the mouth, refusal to move).

**Toxicity is highest at flowering and seed stage** — late summer through first frost. That's August–October in your area, which lines up exactly with the back end of your finishing window.

**Why it matters especially for finishing cattle:**

- Cattle on grain are already at higher risk of respiratory and metabolic stress
- They're confined to smaller areas, so they encounter the same plants repeatedly
- They tend to seek shade more in summer heat — exactly where perilla grows
- Hot, stressed cattle drink more and breathe harder, amplifying the lung damage

**What to do:**

1. **Walk your finishing pen this weekend** before turning the steers in. Check shaded areas, fence lines, any tree-shaded loafing spots.
2. **Pull it by hand** if it's small patches (wear gloves — the oils can irritate skin)
3. **Spray with 2,4-D or glyphosate** for larger infestations — most effective when plants are young and actively growing (now through June)
4. **Mow before it flowers** if spraying isn't an option — toxicity skyrockets at the reproductive stage

Most glyphosate labels (Roundup PowerMAX, Roundup Custom, generic glyphosate products) actually have **no grazing restriction** for non-lactating beef cattle on pasture and rangeland applications. You can technically turn them back in immediately.

**1. Wait 7–14 days minimum, regardless of label.**

Two reasons:
- **Glyphosate needs time to translocate** through the perilla and kill it down to the roots. If you turn cattle in too soon, they'll trample or eat plants that haven't finished dying, and you've wasted the spray.
- **Dying perilla may actually be MORE palatable** for a brief window. As the plant wilts and the strong minty smell fades, cattle become more willing to eat it — and the toxin is still present. This is a documented risk with herbicide treatment of toxic plants.

**2. Wait until plants are visibly dead and dried — ideally 14–21 days.**

Perilla should be brown, crispy, and clearly dead before cattle have access. At that point the ketone toxicity has largely degraded along with the plant tissue, and cattle won't be tempted to eat dead, dry stalks.

- **Apr 27 – May 10:** Keep steers out of treated areas (use temporary electric fence if needed to block off sprayed sections)
