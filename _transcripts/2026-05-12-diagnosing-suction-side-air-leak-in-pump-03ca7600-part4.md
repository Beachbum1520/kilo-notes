## How CIPP works

For context on what your daughter's contractor did:
- A felt or fiberglass liner saturated with thermosetting resin is inverted (turned inside-out) into the existing pipe
- Air or water pressure pushes the liner against the pipe walls
- Heat or UV cures the resin, creating a hard pipe-within-a-pipe
- Result: a new structural pipe inside the old damaged one, no excavation needed

The technology is mature for sewer lines, water mains, and industrial piping. It's used routinely in municipal infrastructure work.

## Why it's hard for pool plumbing

Several problems scale poorly to 1.5" pool suction lines:

**1. Pipe size**

CIPP is generally used for **3" diameter and larger**. The robotics, the liner installation, the cure equipment — all get progressively more difficult as pipes get smaller. 1.5" is at or below the lower limit of what most CIPP contractors will touch.

Some specialized small-diameter CIPP exists (some manufacturers go down to 2"), but 1.5" is genuinely rare.

**2. Elbows and fittings**

CIPP works best on long straight runs with gentle bends. Your pool suction line has:
- A 90° elbow at the pad
- Horizontal run under deck/gazebo
- Another 90° at the skimmer
- Possibly other fittings

Tight 90° elbows are problematic for liner installation. The liner has to navigate the bend without bunching or thinning, and the resulting lined elbow may have reduced flow area or weak spots. Two tight 90s in series compound the difficulty.

**3. Access points**

CIPP installation requires access at both ends of the pipe section being lined. For your situation:
- Pump end: accessible (you've got the pad opening)
- Skimmer end: accessible (through the skimmer body from above)

That part is actually OK for your setup. But the access has to be sized to fit the installation equipment, which for small-diameter work is still bigger than what you can fit through a basket-sized skimmer opening with cure equipment attached.

**4. Flow restriction**

The liner reduces the effective inside diameter of the pipe. For 1.5" pipe with even a 1/8" liner thickness, you lose meaningful cross-sectional area. Your pump has to work harder, you lose flow rate, the SWG sees less flow.

For a sewer line (gravity flow, large pipe), this is acceptable. For a pool suction line where flow matters, the restriction is more painful.

**5. Cost vs. value**

Even if you find a contractor willing to do it, the mobilization cost (specialized equipment for one small job) typically makes CIPP cost more than $1500-3000 for a residential pool line — sometimes much more. - **Read in shade or indirect light** — direct sun makes the dot harder to see clearly
- **Hold the tube at waist level** — looking straight down, not at an angle

- **Read at the water level on the tube** — not at the top of the mixture

This is essentially the same physics as the off/on cycling we discussed — soil saturation around the leak prevents air ingestion. You're just doing it manually and immediately rather than waiting for the leak itself to saturate soil from the inside.

## How to do it

**Setup:**
1. Get the pump into its "sucking major air" state — wait until you're seeing significant bubbling at the SWG cell
2. Have a garden hose ready at the dig site
3. Have eyes on the SWG cell (or a helper watching) so you can see the change in real time

**Execute:**
1. Start filling the hole with water from the garden hose
2. Fill enough to fully submerge the elbow and both glue joints
3. Keep the hose running to maintain water level (water will seep into surrounding soil)
4. Watch the SWG cell

**Read the result:**

**Air ingestion stops within 1-2 minutes** → leak is at the exposed elbow. You've localized it definitively.

**No change after 5+ minutes** → leak is NOT at the exposed elbow. It's somewhere else on the buried run, inaccessible to your water flooding.

**Partial reduction but not elimination** → there's a leak at the elbow, but possibly also another leak elsewhere on the line. Or the water isn't fully reaching the crack.

## Important caveats

**1. The water has to actually reach the crack.**

If the crack is on the underside of the pipe or on a side you can't see, water needs to surround the whole fitting. Fill the hole high enough that the elbow is submerged on all sides, not just topped with water.

**2. Soil drainage matters.**

If your red Georgia clay drains quickly, water you add will percolate away. Keep the hose running to maintain submersion. The test needs sustained water contact at the crack location.

**3. Watch the cell, not the hole.**

The hole getting wet is not the diagnostic signal — the cell clearing of air is. Don't get distracted by the hose work; have the cell visible or have someone watching it.

**4. The reverse test for cleanliness.**

If you want to be thorough: once the cell clears with water in the hole, turn off the hose and let the water drain away. Air ingestion should return as the soil dries. If it returns, you've confirmed the elbow is the source.

## A more sensitive version of the test

Instead of just water, use **dye in the water** — food coloring or a tracer dye. Then:

- Fill the hole with dyed water while pump is running and pulling air
- If the crack is at the exposed elbow, dyed water gets pulled in
- Eventually you might see dyed water emerge at the pump basket (look in the clear lid)

This takes longer and dye showing up at the pump is slow, but it's confirmation if you have any doubt.

## What this test cannot tell you

It can only confirm or rule out **the exposed location specifically**. If the air clears, you know the leak is at the elbow. If it doesn't clear, you only know the leak is NOT at the elbow — it could be anywhere else on the buried run.

So a positive result is highly diagnostic. A negative result eliminates one candidate but leaves others.

## When to do it

The test only works when the pump is actively ingesting air. Based on your cycling pattern:

- After 2-3 hours of continuous running (when air ingestion is heavy)
- Not right after a restart (line is in saturation cycle, no air anyway)

Wait until the next "bad" period during your cycle, then run the test.

During normal filter operation, the multiport valve directs flow:
