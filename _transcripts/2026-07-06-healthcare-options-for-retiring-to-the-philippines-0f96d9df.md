Keeping this mobile-friendly but detailed enough to address the real complexity of his situation.The realistic play is a three-layer stack, and your timing at 55 actually matters more than the plan choice.

**Layer 1 — PhilHealth.** Retirees registered through the Philippine Retirement Authority (SRRV) pay about ₱15,000/year (~$250). It works on fixed case rates with capped benefits, so it rarely covers a full private hospital bill — think of it as a discount, not insurance.

**Layer 2 — Local HMO** (Maxicare, Intellicare, Pacific Cross) for routine care and cashless clinic access. Cheap, but networks are restricted, benefits capped, and age restrictions bite — many cut off new enrollment in your 60s.

**Layer 3 — International health insurance** (Cigna Global, Allianz, AXA, William Russell, Pacific Cross Blue Royale) for the catastrophic tier: major surgery, cancer, and evacuation to Singapore or Bangkok. Budget roughly $150–400/month, rising with age.

The hard truths you should factor in:

**Age pricing is the trap.** By your mid-60s you'll pay 3–4x what a 30-year-old pays for the same policy, and combined with pre-existing conditions and ~12% medical inflation, many expats get priced out and end up self-funding risk. Counterintuitive move: consider locking in an international policy *before* you retire — insurers underwrite you at application, and anything that develops later (say, that inter-arm BP gradient turning into a documented vascular finding) becomes a pre-existing exclusion if you wait.

**Location dictates everything.** Manila (St. Luke's, The Medical City) and Cebu (Chong Hua) are genuinely good. Outside those hubs, doctor density drops from ~29 per 10,000 in Metro Manila to ~2 in places like Palawan, and medevac without coverage runs $15,000–50,000. Your beach-town retirement vision needs to be within striking distance of Cebu or Manila, or evacuation cover is non-negotiable.

**Medicare stops at the border.** It doesn't cover you abroad, so your US safety net is gone unless you fly back — worth keeping Part A (free) as a fallback.

**Cash upfront is real.** Private hospitals almost always require cash deposits upfront, even for insured patients — keep a ₱300–500K liquid buffer regardless of coverage.

Given your health-optimization profile and clean labs, you're an attractive underwriting candidate *now*. That advantage erodes every year you wait.
