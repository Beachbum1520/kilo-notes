Payout certainty for term life comes down to two things, neither of which is "Ethos vs. Midland" as brands: the financial strength of the actual carrier holding the risk, and whether your application was accurate (contestability).

**Who actually pays the claim:**
- Midland National — you're contracting directly with them. A+ (Superior) from AM Best.
- Ethos isn't an insurer, it's a broker/agency. Your term policy is issued by one of its partner carriers — most often Banner Life (formerly Legal & General America), now a subsidiary of Meiji Yasuda Life Insurance Company after completing its acquisition from Legal & General America. AM Best downgraded Banner Life's rating to A (Excellent) from A+ (Superior) in March 2026, reflecting the ownership transition rather than any deterioration in balance sheet strength, with a stable outlook. You don't get to pick which carrier you land with — Ethos routes you based on age, state, coverage amount, and health.

So on raw claims-paying strength, Midland National (A+, direct) edges out an Ethos-placed Banner Life policy (A, one notch down, plus an extra layer between you and the carrier). Both are still solid, established insurers — this isn't a "will they scam you" difference, it's a one-notch AM Best difference plus who you're contracting with.

**What actually determines a real-world payout, regardless of carrier:**
1. **Contestability period** — nearly all US term policies have a 2-year window where the insurer can investigate and deny a claim for material misrepresentation on the application. After 2 years (as long as premiums were paid), denial is essentially only for fraud. Be scrupulously accurate on the app — Ethos's short-form, no-exam underwriting makes it *easier* to accidentally omit something that later surfaces in a death claim's records pull.
2. **Complaint index** — a better predictor of "will they pay smoothly" than the sales pitch. Banner's NAIC complaint index stands at 0.16 against a national baseline of 1.00 — that's good. Check Midland National's on the NAIC site too before you buy.
3. **Full underwriting vs. simplified/no-exam** — a fully underwritten policy (medical exam, records pulled up front) has less contestability risk later, since the insurer already verified your health at issue. No-exam accelerated policies are faster and more convenient but shift more of the risk-verification to the claims stage.

**Bottom line:** if payout certainty is your top priority over convenience, go with a fully underwritten term policy from a top-rated direct carrier (Midland National, or better-priced A++ options like Banner Life direct, Protective, or Prudential) rather than Ethos's no-exam route — same underlying carriers in some cases, but full underwriting removes the main lever an insurer has to contest a claim.

Yes, it'll affect your rate class, but likely not as much as you'd think — and this is exactly the scenario an independent agent earns their keep on.

**How it typically plays out:** Losartan is a common, well-regarded ARB for blood pressure — it's not a red flag drug. Most carriers won't disqualify you from their best rate class just for taking it, but many do require your *controlled* blood pressure reading (on the medication) to fall under a specific threshold to still qualify for Preferred Plus or Preferred. Your recent readings — 118/75 average, 114/80 average — are solidly normal, so if that's consistent at your medical exam, you're in good shape.

**Where it gets carrier-specific:** some insurers automatically bump anyone taking BP medication down a rate tier regardless of how well-controlled it is (treating the diagnosis itself as the risk factor), while others rate purely on the achieved numbers and treat medication as a neutral or even positive sign of managed risk. That's a real, meaningful pricing difference for the exact same health profile — which is the point I made about an independent agent: they'll know which of Banner, Pacific, or Protective (or others) treats controlled hypertension on a single low-dose medication most favorably, and place your application there first instead of you guessing and taking a hit on a formal decline or downgrade that then shows up on your MIB record for future applications.

**One practical thing worth doing before you apply:** get a written confirmation from your doctor of your current diagnosis code and the reason you started the medication (borderline/pre-hypertension vs. diagnosed hypertension is a different code and can matter), and have 2-3 recent readings handy. Agents can often get a "trial offer" — an informal read from an underwriter based on your medical facts — before you commit to a formal application, so you're not spending a contestability-period clock or an inquiry on a guess.
