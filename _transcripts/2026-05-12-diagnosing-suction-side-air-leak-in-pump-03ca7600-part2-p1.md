k (we haven't ruled it out yet). Replacing it with a properly-made union eliminates that variable.

2. **Flex PVC at a threaded adapter is a known weakness.** Flex PVC has slightly different gluing characteristics than rigid PVC, and it can creep under load over time. A threaded plastic-to-plastic connection with flex PVC as the load on one side is more prone to slow failure than a glued union joint would be. The union puts the threaded connection in a more controlled mechanical context (the union body provides rigidity).

3. **One joint is better than two.** With Option B, you have both a threaded adapter joint AND a glued union joint in series. Each is a potential leak point. Option A consolidates to one connection per type (threaded into pump, glue at flex PVC).

4. **You're already cutting either way.** Option B requires cutting the flex PVC. Option A requires cutting the flex PVC AND unscrewing the adapter. The "and unscrewing the adapter" part is 30 seconds of effort. Not a meaningful additional cost.

## Specific install tips for Option A

**For the threaded connection into the pump:**

- Clean the pump threads with a rag — remove any leftover Teflon, dope, or silicone residue from your previous install
- Inspect the pump threads carefully for any hairline cracks (especially if you wrestled with the adapter when installing — over-tightening can crack the female threads in the pump body)
- **3-4 wraps of Teflon tape, wound clockwise** (so it tightens with the direction of threading)
- Apply a thin coat of **pool-rated thread sealant** (Rectorseal T+2 or similar) over the Teflon
- Skip the silicone exterior bead this time — it doesn't help, can hide leaks, and makes future disassembly harder
- Thread in hand-tight, then **1 to 1.5 turns with a strap wrench**. Stop. Plastic pump housings are easy to crack.

**For the flex PVC slip joint:**

- Flex PVC needs special cement or "flex PVC compatible" all-purpose cement. **Regular standard PVC cement works on flex PVC**, but use a generous coat — flex PVC absorbs glue more readily than rigid pipe. Many pros prefer **Christy's Red Hot Blue Glue** or **Weld-On 705** for flex PVC connections.
- Prime both the flex PVC end and the union socket
- Apply cement generously to both surfaces
- Push together firmly, hold 30 seconds
- Don't twist flex PVC after insertion — it doesn't recover like rigid pipe does
- Let cure 2 hours minimum before pressurizing

**Sequence the install carefully:**

You'll want to glue the union's slip end to the flex PVC **before** threading the union body into the pump, OR the other way around — whichever gives you better access. My recommendation:

1. **First**: Thread the union's MPT end into the pump, snug to final torque
2. **Then**: Dry-fit the slip end onto the flex PVC, mark the depth
3. **Then**: Disassemble the union (unscrew the collar), pull the slip half off the flex PVC
4. **Then**: Prime and glue the slip half onto the flex PVC
5. **Finally**: Reassemble the union, tighten collar hand-tight

This way you glue with the slip half off the pump, which gives you room to work and ensures proper alignment.

## On the silicone bead

Just to address this directly: skip the exterior silicone this time. Here's why:

- It doesn't actually seal anything that the Teflon + dope don't already seal
- If the Teflon + dope have a problem, the silicone hides it from you visually (you can't see weeping or staining at the joint)
- It makes the joint look "fixed" when it might not be
- It bonds poorly to PVC and pool chemicals, so it deteriorates and flakes off over time
- It complicates future disassembly

A properly-applied Teflon + thread sealant joint on clean threads with proper torque is air-tight. If yours isn't, the fix is to redo the Teflon/dope/torque — not to bandaid with silicone on top.

- Matching weatherproof receptacle: $20-40
- Result: pump unplugs from power as easily as a lamp

**Option 2: Disconnect switch at the pad**

A weatherproof **disconnect box** mounted on a post or wall next to the pump. Has a pull-out fuse holder or lever-operated switch. You wire from the breaker → disconnect → pump.

This doesn't unplug the pump, but it gives you a local "OFF" that completely isolates the pump from power. You don't have to walk to the main breaker every time you service it. Often code-required for pool equipment regardless.

- $30-80 depending on amperage rating
- Search: "pool pump disconnect switch" or "60 amp non-fused disconnect"

**Option 3: Both — quick-disconnect cord + local disconnect switch**

The professional install. Disconnect switch on the wall, twist-lock receptacle below it, twist-lock cord from receptacle to pump. Now you can:
- Flip the disconnect for safety isolation
- Unplug the cord to physically remove the pump
- Service everything without touching the main breaker

This is what a good pool electrician would install. ~$80-150 in parts total.

## Pool pump electrical specifics to verify first

Before ordering, check:

**1. Voltage** — Is your pump 115V or 230V? Look at the motor nameplate or the dipswitch on the motor. This determines the plug/receptacle type:

- **115V (single-pole)**: NEMA 5-15 (standard outlet) or NEMA L5-20 (twist lock)
- **230V (two-pole)**: NEMA 6-15, 6-20, or L6-20 (twist lock) — most inground pool pumps

**2. Amperage** — From the motor nameplate. Determines the rating of cord and receptacle:
- 1.5HP pump: typically 8-12 amps at 230V
- A 20A circuit/receptacle is standard
- Don't undersize

**3. SWG controller** — Your CircuPool SWG controller is wired into the pump's power feed (the SWG only generates when pump is running, sensed via current through the controller). Don't disrupt that wiring without understanding it. You may need to add the quick-disconnect **between the SWG controller and the pump motor**, not upstream of the SWG.

**4. Bonding** — Pool equipment requires an **equipotential bonding grid** — heavy copper wire (#8 AWG bare) connecting pump motor, SWG, filter (if metal), and other metallic equipment together and to a bonding lug on each device. The bonding wire is NOT the same as the ground wire and must stay continuous. Any quick-disconnect you add should not break the bonding wire. Bonding is bolted directly to a lug on each piece of equipment, separate from the power wiring.

## Important code and safety considerations

**Bonding wire stays continuous.** Your pool equipment has a #8 AWG solid copper bonding wire connecting the pump, SWG, and other metallic components. This wire is NOT the ground wire and does NOT go through your new disconnect. It stays bolted directly to the bonding lug on the pump motor. When you remove the pump, you disconnect the bonding lug separately (one bolt).

**Code compliance.** NEC requires a disconnect within sight of the pump that's at least 5 feet horizontally from the pool's edge. If your timer already serves as a disconnect, you're fine. If you only have a remote disconnect (like at the breaker panel), the twist-lock-in-a-box can serve as the local disconnect — but check your local code. Some jurisdictions require a specific "disconnect switch" rather than a "plug and receptacle."

**Twist-lock vs straight-blade.** Use twist-lock (NEMA L-series). Straight blade plugs (regular plug shape) can vibrate loose, and pool pumps vibrate. Twist-lock physically locks the plug in place — it can't accidentally pull out or work loose.

**Weatherproof rating.** Anything outdoors needs to be NEMA 3R or better. Look for "wet location rated" or "outdoor rated" on the receptacle, box, and plug. Some twist-lock receptacles have a flip-up weatherproof cover ("while-in-use" cover) — get that style.

**Wire gauge.** Whatever you splice through the new box must be at least as heavy as the wire already in the run. Don't reduce wire gauge to fit a smaller plug.

## Why I suggested compression couplings for underground work

The recommendation was based on practical constraints of working in a hole in the ground, not on inherent superiority of the technology:

**1. Glue requires clean, dry surfaces**

PVC primer and cement need:
- Pipe surfaces free of dirt, mud, and debris
- Reasonably dry (some "wet/dry" cements tolerate residual moisture, but none love it)
- Adequate working room to apply primer and glue evenly to the full socket depth
- Time to set without disturbance

In an underground hole:
- Dirt falls in constantly
- Standing water from the line draining is common
- The pipe ends are 6-12 inches below grade with mud around them
- You're working in a cramped, awkward position

Getting a perfectly clean, dry glue joint underground is harder than on a bench. Glue failures in buried plumbing are usually traced to contaminated joints.

**2. Glue requires precise pipe alignment**

When you cut out an elbow and glue in a new one:
- The two existing pipe ends are fixed in place (they're buried, they can't move)
- The new fitting + couplings have to span exactly the gap between them
- Standard slip couplings don't have any "give" — the pipe has to slide all the way into the socket
- If your cut isn't perfect, or the pipes have shifted, you can't get the geometry to work

In a fresh install above ground, you'd flex the pipes to make it work. Underground with rigid PVC, you can't flex.

**3. Compression couplings solve both problems**

Compression repair couplings (also called "slip fix" couplings or "Dresser-style" couplings):
- Slide onto the pipe with the compression nuts loose
- Don't require glue, primer, or pristine surfaces
- The internal rubber gasket seals against the pipe outer surface
- Tighten the nuts and you're done
- Tolerate some pipe end roughness and minor contamination
- Can be slid along the pipe and then "locked" in place, which makes alignment easier in tight quarters

The cost: $10-20 each instead of $1-2 for a slip coupling.

## Why glue is still the traditional and often-preferred choice

**1. Strength and durability**

A properly glued PVC joint is chemically fused — the primer softens the plastic and the cement creates a permanent solvent weld. The joint becomes essentially one continuous piece of plastic, stronger than the pipe itself.

Compression couplings rely on:
- A rubber gasket compressed against the pipe surface
- Mechanical clamping force from the nut

The gasket can degrade over decades. The clamping force can loosen if the pipe shifts or settles. Glue doesn't have either failure mode.

**2. Long-term reliability**

Glued PVC joints, if done properly, last the lifetime of the pipe (50+ years). Compression fittings have a more uncertain long-term track record, especially underground where you can't inspect or service them.

**3. Cost**

Glue + slip couplings: ~$5 total for the whole repair
Compression couplings: $20-40 for two

**4. Lower profile**

Compression couplings are physically larger (thicker walls, the compression nut adds diameter) than slip couplings. In a tight underground space, the bigger fitting can be awkward.

**5. What pool builders actually use**

Professional pool plumbing is almost exclusively glued PVC. Compression fittings are seen as "field repair" or "homeowner DIY" — functional but not the standard. If you ever sold the house and a pool inspector saw compression fittings underground, they'd note it.

## The honest tradeoff for your situation

**Glue if you can:**
- Cleaner permanent install
- Cheaper
- More confidence in long-term reliability
- Aligns with how the rest of your pool is plumbed

**Compression if you must:**
- Work area is too cramped to glue cleanly
- Can't get pipe ends clean and dry
- Pipe alignment is awkward and you need the adjustability
- You're not confident in your gluing skill on this particular joint

- Slide the slip fix back over to span the gap
