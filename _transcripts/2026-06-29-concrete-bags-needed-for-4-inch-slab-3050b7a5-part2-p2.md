### CLAUDE (2026-07-04T22:22)
I'd steer you off dry-setting for *these* specifically, and it comes down to what these posts have to do. Let me give you the straight version, because dry-set has its place — this just isn't it.

**Where dry-set is fine:** fence posts, mailbox posts, a gate post — anything where the concrete's mostly there to keep the post from wiggling and it's not carrying a real structural load. Pour dry mix in around the post, let ground moisture wick up and set it over days. Easy, no mixing. For a line of field-fence posts I wouldn't blink at it.

**Why not your barn posts:** these are carrying a roof, and a roof in wind wants to *lift* the posts out of the ground. That makes it a real structural connection, and dry-set has two weaknesses that matter exactly here:

The first is **incomplete, unreliable hydration.** Dry mix depends on water finding its way through the whole column from the surrounding soil. In a 4-foot-deep hole in Georgia clay — which sheds water rather than soaking it in — the bottom of that column may never fully hydrate. You can end up with strong concrete up top and crumbly, half-set powder down deep, and you can't see it. The deep part is the part resisting uplift, so that's the worst place to have weak concrete.

The second is **cure time and strength.** Even when it does hydrate, dry-set reaches lower ultimate strength and gets there slower than properly mixed concrete. For posts that need to hold a roof against wind, you want the full strength you paid for.

**What I'd do instead, in order of preference:**

**Wet-set** — mix it (you've got the tractor mixer, or mix in the wheelbarrow) and pour it wet around each braced post. This is the right way for structural posts. Strongest, most reliable, and you *know* it's hydrated all the way down. It's more work per hole, but it's six holes, not sixty.

**Dry-pour with forced water** — if you really want to skip mixing, at least don't trust the soil. Pour the dry mix in **lifts of about a foot**, and soak each lift with water from a hose until it's saturated before adding the next. This gets you most of the way to proper hydration and is far better than dry-and-wait. Still not as good as wet-set, but acceptable.

**Straight dry-and-wait** — the one I'd avoid here.

There's also a **crushed-stone / no-concrete option** some builders now prefer for wood posts — backfill the hole with compacted angular gravel in lifts instead of concrete. It drains water away from the post (concrete can hold moisture against wood and rot it), locks the post tight when compacted, and it's what a lot of post-frame engineers now spec. But it demands good compaction in lifts and the right angular stone, and for a first-timer, wet-set concrete with the post isolated is the more foolproof structural choice.

My bottom line: **wet-set them.** It's six holes. Brace each post plumb, mix and pour wet, put a tamped concrete pad or stone in the hole bottom first for bearing, and let them cure before you frame. The extra hour of mixing buys you a barn that won't lift or lean, and you've already got the mixer. Save dry-set for the day you're running a fence line.
