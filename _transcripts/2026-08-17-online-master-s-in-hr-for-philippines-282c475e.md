Then I'd push back on the premise: a master's is probably the wrong instrument for that problem.

In the Philippine HR market, entry and mid-level hiring screens on **experience and specific compliance/tool knowledge**, not graduate degrees. A fresh master's holder with no HR track record often gets *fewer* callbacks, not more — recruiters read it as salary expectations above the role. You'd be spending two years and ₱150k–500k to solve a problem the credential doesn't touch.

**Diagnose before you spend.** "Can't find meaningful employment" has at least four different causes with four different fixes:

- Bachelor's is in an unrelated field → the gap is HR-specific credentials, not level
- Degree is from a low-tier school → a master's from UP *would* genuinely fix this
- She's in a province with no HR job market → geography, not credentials
- Resume/targeting/salary expectations are off → free to fix, costs nothing

Only the second one justifies a master's.

**Cheaper levers that actually move PH HR hiring:**

- **Philippine payroll and statutory compliance** — SSS, PhilHealth, Pag-IBIG, BIR withholding, 13th month, Alphalist. This is the single most hireable hard skill for entry-level HR here, and there are certifications for it that cost a fraction of a degree.
- **PMAP certificate programs** — the Certificate in Human Resource Development runs blended online/face-to-face on Saturdays at ₱28,000 for members, and stacks into their Diploma in Human Resource Management. Modular, so she can stop after one.
- **BPO and shared-services HR ops** — highest hiring volume for HR in the country, most willing to take candidates without a pure HR background, and the experience compounds fast.

One thing to rule out now: **CHRP is not available to her.** It requires a minimum of five years of relevant HR professional experience. It's positioned as a mid-career milestone, not an entry-level badge. Anyone selling it to her as an employability fix is selling.

If she does the master's, the right sequence is: get into HR first, then let an employer subsidize the degree. Doing it in the other order is paying full price for a credential that's worth more once she has something to attach it to.

So — what's her bachelor's in, and where is she based? Those two answers determine whether any of this changes.
