### SCOTT (2026-04-11T19:54)
How much beefsteak plant does a pig have to ingest to cause it harm?

### CLAUDE (2026-04-11T19:54)

Good question, and worth knowing given how common perilla mint (beefsteak plant, *Perilla frutescens*) is in the Southeast — including along fence rows and woodland edges exactly like yours in Franklin, GA.

Here's what the research shows:

**Pigs specifically:** The literature on perilla mint toxicity is heavily focused on cattle, sheep, and horses. Most documented cases involve cattle, though other animals may be susceptible. There is no established toxic dose for pigs specifically, and pigs appear to be considerably less susceptible than ruminants. The mechanism — perilla ketones causing atypical interstitial pneumonia (fluid buildup in the lungs) — is particularly damaging to ruminant respiratory physiology.

**No known threshold for pigs:** Little is known about the amount of perilla mint needed to cause detrimental effects in livestock generally, nor about the factors that make some animals more susceptible than others.

**What we do know about dose in cattle (for reference):** A 174-pound calf fed 5 pounds of perilla mint hay in the seed stage died within three days, while a 291-pound calf fed similar amounts in the green (July) stage for 15 days showed no clinical signs. This illustrates how dramatically toxicity varies by plant stage.

**Plant stage matters enormously:** The early pre-seed stage is of relatively low toxicity, while the flowering and green seed stage is most toxic, especially the seeds themselves. The plant is also reported to remain toxic even after being killed by herbicides or dried in hay.

**Bottom line for your operation:** Your pigs are likely at lower risk than your Dexters, but I wouldn't count on it being safe. The prudent move is to control it in any areas your pigs can access — especially shaded fence lines, creek banks, and woodland edges — the same as you would for your cattle. If you ever see a pig showing labored breathing, open-mouth panting, or extended neck posture after being on pasture, call your vet immediately.

### SCOTT (2026-05-03T23:56)
What kills it?

### CLAUDE (2026-05-03T23:56)
 Based on the search results already retrieved, here's what works:

**Herbicides**

*Pre-emergent (before it sprouts):*
- Aminopyralid, dicamba, imazapic, or picloram — applied as soil broadcast before seedling emergence

*Post-emergent (on growing plants):*
- **2,4-D** — effective, especially low-volatile ester formulations
- **Dicamba** — works well, often tank-mixed with 2,4-D (products like Weedmaster, Rifle-D, Brash)
- **Aminopyralid** — (products like GrazonNext, DuraCor)
- **Glyphosate** — effective post-emergent
- **Triclopyr** — (products like Crossbow)
- **Picloram** — effective but watch label restrictions near water

All post-emergent apps should include a **non-ionic surfactant (0.25% v/v)** for best results.

**Critical timing:** Apply when plants are young and actively growing — spring through early flower stage. Once they're tall and flowering, control is significantly less effective.

**Two important caveats:**
1. Remove livestock from treated areas until plants are fully dead — dying perilla mint may actually become *more attractive* to grazing animals
2. The toxin remains viable in dead/dried plant material, so don't assume dead plants are safe

**Mechanical options:**
- Hand-pulling works due to the shallow root system — but must be done **before seed set**
- Mowing is limited in effectiveness since the plant can resprout and still produce seed after cutting

Given how prolific a seed producer it is, one season of control won't solve it — plan for multi-year management.

### SCOTT (2026-05-03T23:57)
Should I use a pre and post on it?

### CLAUDE (2026-05-03T23:57)
 For your situation, yes — a pre + post combination is the most effective approach, but **timing is everything.**

**The practical sequence:**

1. **Now (May):** Hit existing plants with a post-emergent while they're still young and actively growing. This is your best window — control drops off sharply once they start flowering (July+).

2. **Next spring (April):** Follow up with a pre-emergent before new seedlings emerge from the seed bank.

**Why both matter for your farm specifically:**

Perilla mint is a prolific seed producer, so even a perfect kill this year leaves a seed bank that will sprout next season. The pre-emergent breaks that cycle. One year of post-emergent only and you'll be fighting it again.

**Practical consideration for your paddocks:** Since you're running both cattle and pigs in rotational areas, Grazon P+D or GrazonNext (aminopyralid + picloram or 2,4-D) are commonly used on pasture and are labeled for grazing ground. Glyphosate is non-selective, so you'd only want that on fence lines or non-pasture areas where you don't mind killing grass too.

**One thing to confirm:** Check each product label for any restrictions near the creek banks on your property — some picloram-containing products have buffer requirements near water, which may matter depending on where your perilla mint populations are heaviest.

Worth a call to your local UGA Extension office in Franklin too — they'll know which products and rates are most commonly used in Heard County.

### SCOTT (2026-05-04T00:00)
What’s the difference between Amine 400 and remedy and grayzon?

### CLAUDE (2026-05-04T00:00)
 These are three very different herbicides — different chemistry, different targets, different uses.

---

**Amine 400 (2,4-D amine)**
- Kills broadleaf weeds only, won't harm grasses
- Short soil residual — breaks down relatively quickly
- Very affordable, widely available
- Safe near water in amine formulation (low volatility)
- Good for perilla mint when young, but weaker on mature plants
- Grazing restrictions are short (usually 0–7 days)

---

**Remedy (triclopyr)**
- Broadleaf weeds AND woody brush/trees
- Useful if you have both perilla mint and brush encroachment on the same fence lines
- Longer residual than 2,4-D
- Often tank-mixed with 2,4-D for broader spectrum
- Good on tougher-to-kill plants
- Watch label for water buffers

---

**GrazonNext (aminopyralid + 2,4-D)**
- The heavy hitter for pasture weed management
- Broadest spectrum of the three — kills a wide range of broadleaf weeds
- Longer soil residual (this is also the caution — manure from treated animals can carry aminopyralid and damage gardens/crops)
- Most effective against perilla mint specifically
- More expensive but fewer applications needed

---

**For perilla mint on your farm**, GrazonNext is the most effective single product. Amine 400 is a cheaper option for young plants. Remedy earns its place if you're also battling woody brush along those fence lines and creek banks.

 That's a really common frustration with sweet gum — it's one of the most persistent re-sprouters in the Southeast. The brush hogging is actually working against you a little bit.

**Why they keep coming back so hard:**

Sweet gum develops an extensive root system fast, even as seedlings. When you brush hog them, you're essentially doing the same thing as mowing grass — it stimulates vigorous regrowth from the root. The root system is still fully intact and loaded with energy reserves.

---

**What you need to do differently:**

The spray has to **kill the root**, not just the top. That means:

- **Spray first, brush hog after** — never before. You want green actively growing leaf surface to absorb and translocate the herbicide down to the roots
- Let the herbicide work for **30+ days** before mowing
- Spray when plants are actively growing and not stressed — which right now in May is actually a good window

---

**Product-wise for this situation:**

**Remedy Ultra + Amine 400** at full labeled rate with surfactant is solid. Some Southeast farmers add **Arsenal (imazapyr)** to the mix for really persistent sweet gum pressure — it has soil residual that suppresses regrowth. However Arsenal will suppress grass too, so it's better suited to fence lines than open pasture.

**Realistic expectation:** Even with a good kill spray this spring, you'll likely need one more follow-up treatment next spring on survivors. Two solid years of spray-then-mow (in that order) should break the cycle significantly.
