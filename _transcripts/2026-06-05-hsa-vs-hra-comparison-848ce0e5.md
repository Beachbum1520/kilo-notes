The key differences come down to ownership and control: HSA funds are his to keep and invest, portable if he changes jobs, while HRA is employer-owned and stays with the company; HSA contributions come from him or his employer pre-tax, but HRA is purely employer-funded; and the tax treatment differs fundamentally across contributions, growth, and withdrawals.

For someone like Scott, the real edge is the HSA's triple tax advantage combined with its investment potential and ability to function like a retirement account after 65—but I should push back on one thing: much of what he's spending on (peptides, supplements, DEXA scans) doesn't actually qualify for HSA reimbursement, which is worth flagging as a reality check against the "let it grow" strategy he might be considering.Both are tax-advantaged health funding vehicles, but they're structurally opposite in the dimension that matters most to you: **ownership.** An HSA is your asset; an HRA is your employer's liability.

| | HSA | HRA |
|---|---|---|
| Who owns it | You | Employer |
| Who funds it | You + employer (pre-tax) | Employer only |
| Requires HDHP | Yes (2026: ≥$1,700 self / $3,400 family deductible) | No |
| 2026 contribution cap | $4,400 self / $8,750 family (+$1,000 catch-up at 55) | Varies by type; EBHRA capped at $2,200 |
| Investable / grows | Yes, tax-free | No |
| Portable at job change | Yes, forever | Generally forfeited |
| Tax profile | Triple: pre-tax in, tax-free growth, tax-free qualified withdrawal | Reimbursements tax-free to you; no deduction, no growth |

The HSA's triple-tax structure is genuinely rare in the code — contributions are pre-tax, the money grows tax-free, and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. There's no other account that does all three. After 65, non-medical withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income with no penalty, so it functionally degrades into a traditional IRA at worst — meaning there's no downside to over-funding it relative to a 401(k).

For your situation specifically, three things worth pushing on:

**The high-earner play is receipt-banking, not reimbursement.** At your income, the optimal move isn't using the HSA to pay current medical bills — it's max-funding it, paying medical costs out of taxable cash, investing the HSA aggressively, and saving receipts indefinitely. You can reimburse yourself tax-free decades later for any qualified expense incurred after the account opened. That converts the HSA into a stealth retirement account with better tax treatment than your 401(k). The HRA can't do any of this — it's use-it-or-lose-the-relationship.

**Your actual health spend largely doesn't qualify — and this is the sharp limit on the strategy.** Peptides, TRT, IGF-1, and tirzepatide are reimbursable *only* with a prescription and a legitimate diagnostic indication; cosmetic/performance/longevity framing disqualifies them. Supplements (fish oil, magnesium, glycine, PS, DHEA) are almost entirely non-qualified absent a documented medical necessity letter. DEXA and labs qualify when diagnostic. So if your mental model is "HSA funds my optimization stack," that's mostly wrong — but it doesn't undermine the receipt-banking case, since that case doesn't depend on spending the money on health at all.

**The choice is usually not actually yours.** HSA eligibility is gated by HDHP enrollment, and you can't contribute to an HSA while covered by a general-purpose HRA — they conflict. So the real decision happens at open enrollment when you pick the plan. If your employer offers an HDHP+HSA path, that almost always beats a richer plan + HRA for someone in a high bracket who can absorb the deductible from cash flow, because you're trading a small expected-cost increase for a uniquely tax-advantaged investment vehicle you keep forever.

One caveat to stress-test against your own plans: if you're targeting an exit to Southeast Asia in your 60s, the HSA's qualified-expense rules are US-centric — foreign medical care can qualify, but documentation and currency conversion get messy, and you'd want to verify the custodian's posture before leaning on it as an offshore healthcare fund.
