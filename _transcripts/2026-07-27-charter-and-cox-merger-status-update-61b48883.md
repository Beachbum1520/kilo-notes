## Two explanations produce the same ask

A scaling plan is also a migration blueprint. If you hand it over without framing, you don't control which one it becomes.

You can't distinguish these from the ask itself. You can only distinguish them by forcing someone to answer the scope question in writing.

## Scope is the entire ballgame

Note the asymmetry in the FCC language. The comparator Charter cites is its "100% U.S.-based **customer sales and service** employee workforce" — narrow. But the actual obligation is to onshore all of the job functions currently handled off-shore by Cox within 18 months, and per the order's footnotes, Charter must onshore all of Cox's overseas employees. That's broad, and it says *Cox*, not *Cox residential*.

Blueprint RF is a Cox Communications company. On a plain reading of the order, your Philippines headcount is in scope. The "separate entity" framing is precisely the argument that it isn't — which tells you the argument is being made, not that it's been won.

Two live ambiguities worth pressing:
- **Are TDE/engineering/NOC functions "customer sales and service"?** Arguably no. That's your strongest ground, and it's stronger for TDE than for the call center.
- **Do Cloudstaff contractors count as "Cox's overseas employees"?** They aren't Cox employees. That's the obvious arbitrage — and it's exactly the gap Winfrey closed rhetorically on Friday when he said onshore *and insource*. Don't build a plan that depends on that loophole surviving contact with a compliance function.

## Don't optimize the structure — resolve the scope

There's no clever structure that survives both conditions. Onshoring kills the location; insourcing kills the vendor model. If BPRF is in scope, everything offshore goes to zero within 18 months of close regardless of how you paper it. So the only question worth spending capital on is in-or-out, and you want that answered before you scale, not at month 15 when someone runs the inventory and your team is 40% larger than it is today.

**Concretely:** deliver the plan, but lead it with the assumption stated explicitly. *"This plan assumes BPRF's Philippines operations are outside the scope of the FCC onshoring condition, on the basis that these are B2B technical functions, not consumer sales and service. If that assumption is incorrect, the alternative plan is X, at a cost of Y and a timeline of Z."*

That does three things: it protects you, it forces the scope question up to someone with authority to answer it, and it makes the answer a document rather than a hallway conversation. It also costs you nothing — it reads as rigor, not resistance, which is exactly the register you want pre-close.

**And hedge the build:** scale via Cloudstaff contract terms with short notice periods, not via anything that creates lease, entity, or severance liability. If scope goes against you, you want the unwind to be a contract amendment, not a restructuring.

Charter is paying $3.5 billion cash at close for Cox's commercial fiber and managed IT assets, has raised its synergy target toward $1 billion and cut its leverage target to prioritize debt paydown. That is a company that will be looking hard at small non-core lines within two quarters of close.

If BPRF gets shopped, you're closer to the front of that conversation than to the back of it — and it's a materially different endgame than "survive the integration."

Worth checking whether a divestiture is a *separate* trigger in your LTIP language from a change of control at close. Those often behave differently, and you've already flagged the involuntary-termination question as unresolved. If a sale of BPRF is now a live scenario, that's a third case you don't have an answer for.

What does TDE cover in your org — is it customer-facing at all, or purely design and deployment? That distinction is the load-bearing fact in the scope argument, and it's the one thing you can characterize before anyone else does.
