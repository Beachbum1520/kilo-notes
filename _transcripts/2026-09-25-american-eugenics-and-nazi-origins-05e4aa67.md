Mostly true, but "that's where Hitler got his ideas" overstates it. The American movement was a major **model and legitimizer** for Nazi race policy, not its origin.

**What holds up:**
- The US ran the world's most aggressive eugenics program in the early 1900s. About 30 states passed forced-sterilization laws, and roughly 60,000 or more people were sterilized.
- *Buck v. Bell* (1927) had the Supreme Court uphold forced sterilization, with Holmes writing "three generations of imbeciles are enough."
- Harry Laughlin's "model sterilization law" was a template for Germany's 1933 sterilization law. Heidelberg gave him an honorary degree in 1936.
- In *Mein Kampf*, Hitler praised US immigration restriction, and the 1924 Immigration Act was built on eugenic arguments. He also reportedly called Madison Grant's *The Passing of the Great Race* his "bible."
- Nazi lawyers studied Jim Crow and anti-miscegenation laws while drafting the Nuremberg Laws. James Whitman's *Hitler's American Model* covers this.
- US money, including Rockefeller Foundation grants, funded German eugenics institutes.
- At Nuremberg, defendants cited *Buck v. Bell* in their defense.

**Where the popular version breaks down:**
- Eugenics started in Britain with Francis Galton in 1883, not in America.
- Germany had its own "racial hygiene" movement from the 1890s, led by Alfred Ploetz, independent of the US.
- Nazi antisemitism came from centuries of European tradition and völkisch nationalism. The US didn't supply it.
- The Nazis went far past sterilization, to the T4 killings of disabled people and then genocide. America had no equivalent.

The podcast was likely drawing on Edwin Black's *War Against the Weak*, which makes the strong version of this argument. The accurate framing is that the US gave the Nazis legal blueprints, scientific respectability, and a precedent from a democracy. The ideology itself was already homegrown in Germany.

That detail pins down the contest, but I still can't find a solo show that covers it.

The contest was the **1945 "Search for Norma."** The Cleveland Plain Dealer and the Cleveland Health Museum invited women to submit body measurements matching "Norma," a statue on display at the museum, with war bonds as prizes. More than 3,800 women entered, and none of them actually matched Norma's measurements. The statues were Norma and Normman, made in 1943 by sculptor Abram Belskie and gynecologist Dr. Robert Dickinson. The eugenics link is direct: the male data came from Charles Davenport, founder of the Eugenics Record Office, and Harry Shapiro, who analyzed the figures, later led the American Eugenics Association.

The podcast may have gotten one detail wrong: I don't think the statues are at the Smithsonian. They were first shown at the American Museum of Natural History, then went to Cleveland. A related 1921 eugenics statue, the "Average Young American Male," is at Harvard Medical School's Countway Library. The show may have mixed these up, or I may be wrong about where Norma and Normman ended up.
