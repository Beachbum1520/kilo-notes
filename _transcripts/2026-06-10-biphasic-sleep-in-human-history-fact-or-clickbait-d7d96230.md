The real research is solid; the "all humans, all of history" packaging is where it tips into clickbait.

The core finding comes from historian Roger Ekirch, who spent years combing through diaries, court records, medical texts, and literature from pre-industrial Europe. He found hundreds of references to "first sleep" and "second sleep" as ordinary, unremarked-on concepts — people would sleep a few hours, wake naturally for an hour or two around midnight, and then return for a second stretch. The waking period wasn't treated as insomnia; it was just part of the night. People prayed, talked, had sex, tended fires, or even visited neighbors. The fact that writers referenced it so casually, without explanation, is the strongest evidence it was a shared cultural norm.

There's also a physiological hook: in the 1990s, sleep researcher Thomas Wehr put subjects in 14 hours of darkness per night for weeks, and many naturally drifted into exactly this segmented pattern — two sleep bouts with a quiet, calm wakeful gap in between. So there's a plausible mechanism, not just old documents.

Where the clickbait creeps in: the strong documentary evidence is mostly Western European and roughly early-modern (medieval through ~1700s), not literally "all of human history" or "every culture on Earth." Sleep patterns historically varied a lot by climate, latitude, and culture — siestas, polyphasic napping, communal sleeping arrangements. And the disappearance is usually pinned on artificial lighting and a clock-driven work culture more than the Industrial Revolution as a single event. Some scholars also push back that Ekirch may have somewhat overgeneralized from the texts.

One thing worth flagging given your own sympathetic-dominant, middle-of-the-night waking pattern: this research is sometimes used to argue that waking at 2–3am isn't inherently pathological — it may be a partial echo of that older rhythm.
