How many bags per pad and how many bags at a time in my mixer?

### CLAUDE (2026-07-03T16:21)

**Per pad:** ~28 bags of the 60 lb to hit 5.5". Call it **28**, and let the low spots in your clay soak up any extra — that's your cushion. Two pads = 56, which is why the 24-plus-36 buy works.

**Per batch in the mixer:** load **2 bags at a time**, not 3. Here's the reasoning, since you're solo:

That 9 cu ft drum *could* physically take 3 bags (1.35 cu ft), but you never fill a concrete mixer near its rated number — the drum needs air space to tumble and fold the mix, and an overloaded drum just sloshes and mixes poorly. Two 60 lb bags is ~0.9 cu ft wet, which tumbles clean and dumps into a wheelbarrow without slopping over. More importantly, **2 bags of wet concrete is ~150 lbs to move per batch** — manageable solo. Three bags is ~225 lbs sloshing in a wheelbarrow, which is how a guy working alone tweaks his back or dumps a load.

So the rhythm per pad:
- **2 bags per batch → ~14 batches per pad**
- Water first (a little in the drum), then the two bags, then dribble in water to target consistency
- Dump, place, go again

Fourteen batches sounds like a lot, but each one's only a couple minutes of mixing, and the steady 2-bag pace is exactly what keeps a solo pour from beating you up. You'll find your groove around batch three.

**Counting so you don't come up short or overpour pad one:** physically set aside 28 bags for today's pad before you start. When that stack's gone, the pad's done — don't dip into tomorrow's pile. If you're tracking batches, 14 batches of 2 = your 28. Easy count to keep in your head even when you're tired.

One consistency note: aim for a mix that's **plastic, not soupy** — holds a soft peak on the trowel, wet enough to place and screed one-handed but not running. Too wet is the temptation when you're tired and want it to pour easy, but it weakens the slab and floats the aggregate. If a batch comes out stiff, add water by the cupful, not the gallon.

So: 28 bags a pad, 2 bags a batch, ~14 batches, set aside today's 28 before you fire the mixer. Go pour.

### SCOTT (2026-07-03T16:37)
Should I put plastic down on the ground underneath it and if so, how many of square feet do I need?

### CLAUDE (2026-07-03T16:37)

This is a real judgment call, and it depends on what you did for a base. Let me give you the honest tradeoff, because plastic under a slab isn't automatically good.

**The case for plastic (a vapor barrier):** it stops ground moisture from wicking up into the slab and, more useful for you, stops your dry concrete from losing its water *downward* into thirsty soil or gravel during cure. Losing water to the base too fast can weaken the bottom of the slab. On a moisture-hungry base it helps the concrete cure stronger.

**The case against, for your situation:** you're pouring on Georgia clay (or clay with a little gravel). A slab poured directly on clay actually *grips* it, and a plastic sheet becomes a slip plane that lets the slab slide and can encourage shrinkage cracks as it cures — because the bottom stays slick-wet while the top dries and shrinks. On outdoor grade-level slabs over clay, a lot of guys skip the plastic for exactly this reason and it's fine.

**What I'd actually do for a livestock waterer pad:** 
- If you put down and tamped a **gravel base** like we talked about — yes, lay plastic, because gravel drains water away from the curing slab fast and the plastic keeps that moisture in the mix where it belongs.
- If you're pouring **straight on the clay** with no gravel — skip the plastic, pour on the clay, and instead focus on **curing the top** (keep it damp with a light water spray or cover the finished surface with plastic for a few days *after* it sets). That's where you actually gain strength.

Either way, the bigger strength win isn't under the slab — it's **curing the top after the pour.** Keep the finished surface damp or covered for 3–5 days and don't let that July sun flash-dry it. That matters far more than what's underneath.

**If you do want plastic under it, square footage:**
- Pad footprint is **27 sq ft each** (65" × 60").
- Buy with overhang so you can handle it and it laps up inside the forms a bit — figure **~7' × 7' per pad = ~50 sq ft each.**
- For both pads, **~104 sq ft.** A standard **10' × 25' roll of 6-mil poly** (250 sq ft) is the common cheap buy and covers both pads with plenty to spare — and the leftover is exactly what you'd use to *cover the top* for curing, which you want regardless.

So my rec: **grab one roll of 6-mil poly** either way. Use it under the slab only if you laid gravel; use it over the top for curing in all cases. One $20–30 roll covers both jobs and both pads.

Did you end up putting gravel down, or are you pouring on the bare clay? That's the deciding factor on the under-slab sheet.
