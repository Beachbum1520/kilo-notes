### CLAUDE (2026-05-07T11:57)
 Yes, you should adjust — a Power Red (double red cell) donation removes about twice the red blood cells of a whole blood donation, and that hits aerobic capacity hard for 1–2 weeks while your hemoglobin rebuilds. Today's planned Z2 run at HR 112–122 is exactly the kind of session that will feel "off" — your HR will run 10–20 bpm higher than normal at the same pace because your blood is carrying less oxygen.

Practical adjustments for the next ~2 weeks:

**Today's run:** Keep the duration but drop pace expectations entirely. Run by HR only (your 122 cap), and expect to be walking or shuffling at 4.0–4.5 mph to stay under it. That's normal and not a sign of detraining.

**Next 7–10 days:** Easy aerobic work is fine and probably helpful, but skip anything threshold, tempo, intervals, or heavy lifting to true failure. Hydrate aggressively (you're down ~500ml of fluid plus the cells) and prioritize iron-rich food.

**Week 2:** You'll likely feel mostly normal but still 5–10% off on hard efforts. Ease back into intensity.

**Full recovery:** Red Cross says 112 days before you can donate again for a reason — that's roughly how long full RBC restoration takes. Most athletes feel "back to baseline" around 3–4 weeks though.

One flag: if you feel lightheaded, unusually breathless, or your HR spikes way above 122 even at a walk today, stop. That's your body telling you it needs another day.

### CLAUDE (2026-05-07T11:59)
 I'd skip it today, and probably for 3–5 days post-donation.

The concern isn't the cold itself — it's the cardiovascular load. Cold plunge causes intense peripheral vasoconstriction, a sharp BP spike, and a cold-shock response that increases cardiac demand. With reduced red cell mass, your heart is already working harder to deliver oxygen, and you're mildly hypovolemic from the fluid loss. Stacking cold shock on top of that is asking your cardiovascular system to handle two stressors it's not optimized for right now.

There's also a fainting risk. Post-donation orthostatic issues are common for 24–48 hours, and the vasoconstriction → vasodilation swing when you exit the plunge can drop your BP fast. Doing that alone in cold water is the part I'd actually worry about.

When to resume: once you've had 2–3 normal-feeling days with stable HR on easy efforts, you're fine to plunge again. If you want something in the meantime, a cool shower (60–70°F) gives you some of the recovery and alertness benefits without the cardiovascular hit.

### CLAUDE (2026-05-07T12:01)
 That makes sense — and it's a smart reason. Elevated hemoglobin/hematocrit raises blood viscosity, which compounds the already-elevated VTE risk from long-haul flights (dehydration, immobility, lower cabin pressure). Manila is ~16+ hours of flight time from most US airports, which puts you well into the high-risk window where prophylactic measures actually matter.

A power red is a pretty effective lever for this — you've dropped your hematocrit by roughly 4–6 percentage points in one session, which is more than you'd get from a whole blood donation. Just keep in mind the timing tradeoff: hematocrit stays suppressed for several weeks, which is exactly what you want for the flight, but it also means the training adjustments we talked about will overlap with your trip prep.

A few things worth considering for the flight itself, since you're already thinking proactively:

Hydration matters more than usual now — aim for clear/pale urine in the 24 hours before and during the flight. Cabin air is brutally dry and you're starting with less plasma volume.

Compression socks (15–20 mmHg is fine, 20–30 if you have a history) are probably the highest-leverage thing after hydration. Walk the aisle every 1–2 hours and do calf pumps at your seat.

Skip alcohol on the flight. It's a vasodilator and diuretic, both bad for this.

If you have any personal or family history of clotting, that's worth a conversation with your doctor about whether aspirin or anything else makes sense for the flight specifically.

### CLAUDE (2026-05-07T12:04)
 Three weeks is a solid block — long enough that you'll want real structure, not just "stay active." A few things worth thinking about:

**Heat and humidity acclimatization.** Manila in late May/early June is hot season transitioning into wet season — expect 88–95°F with 70–85% humidity. That's a significant physiological stressor on top of jet lag and reduced hematocrit. Your HR will run 10–15 bpm higher at any given pace for the first 10–14 days while you acclimatize. Plan to run by HR, not pace, especially the first week. Early morning (5–6 AM) is your friend — temps are tolerable and humidity, while still high, is at least stable.

**Hydration and electrolytes.** You'll sweat dramatically more than at home. Plain water won't cut it for sessions over 45 min — you'll want sodium (500–1000 mg/L) to avoid hyponatremia and the headache/fatigue spiral that comes with it. LMNT, Liquid IV, or just salt + a pinch of potassium in water all work.

**Training facility.** Is your hotel gym usable, or will you need to find something? Manila has decent commercial gyms (Anytime Fitness, Gold's, Elorde) but quality varies a lot by location. Worth scouting before you go if strength work matters to you for those three weeks.

**The hematocrit angle works in reverse on the way home.** You'll be ~5 weeks post-donation by June 9th, so most of your red cell mass will be back, but the long flight home is still long. Same compression sock and hydration protocol applies.

Where in Metro Manila will you be based? That actually matters for what's realistic training-wise — traffic and air quality vary enormously by area.
